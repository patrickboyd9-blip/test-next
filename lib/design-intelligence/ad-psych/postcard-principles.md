# Top 21 Psychology Principles for Direct Mail Postcards

> **BASELINE CRITERIA (hard rule from Patrick):** every mailer the AI generates or recommends must always offer the client a sell-the-outcome option or guidance (benefits and experience over features, as in principle #21). This applies whatever emotion is chosen, fear or joy alike.

Priority verticals: Roofing | We Buy Houses (WBH) / Real Estate | Medicare | Food/Retail Coupons
Last updated: 2026-10-20. Every citation's existence was checked against the Crossref metadata API (title/author/year matched) or the primary source. Numbers in examples are **placeholders to fill with your own verified data**. They are not research findings.
Evidence tags: **[Strong]** = replicated / meta-analytic support; **[Mixed]** = real but smaller, context-dependent, or contested; **[Weak]** = classic study with failed or limited replication.

---
## 1. Attention capture (the front side has seconds)
Big headline, one dominant image, minimal clutter. In eye-tracking of print ads, pictorial elements captured attention independent of size, and text attention rose with its surface size. **[Strong]**
- Roofing: aerial/drone photo of the street. WBH: plain "I'd like to make an offer on [address]". Coupons: large appetizing product photo + price. Medicare: clean, readable layout (see compliance below; no government look-alike design).
- Cite: Pieters, R., & Wedel, M. (2004). Attention capture and transfer in advertising: Brand, pictorial, and text-size effects. *Journal of Marketing, 68*(2), 36–50. https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkg.68.2.36.27794

## 2. Reciprocity
Give first: free inspection, free home valuation, a free item with purchase. **[Mixed]**: favor-driven compliance is well documented in lab settings, but effect sizes in mail are not established.
- Medicare caution: gifts only of nominal value, offered regardless of enrollment, never cash (42 CFR 422.2263(b)(1)–(2)); no meals at all (422.2263(b)(3)).
- Cite: Regan, D. T. (1971). Effects of a favor and liking on compliance. *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 7*(6), 627–639. https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-1031(71)90025-4

## 3. Social proof (descriptive norms)
"Your neighbors on [street] chose us" plus real reviews. Local, similar-other norms outperformed generic ones in a hotel-towel field experiment. **[Mixed]**: the original effect was real, but later replications found smaller effects.
- Only use true counts and real, consented testimonials. Medicare testimonials must come from enrollees, be disclosed if paid, and label actor portrayals (42 CFR 422.2262(b)).
- Cite: Goldstein, N. J., Cialdini, R. B., & Griskevicius, V. (2008). A room with a viewpoint: Using social norms to motivate environmental conservation in hotels. *Journal of Consumer Research, 35*(3), 472–482. https://doi.org/10.1086/586910

## 4. Authority
Licenses, manufacturer certifications, years in business, BBB rating (only if true). **[Mixed]**: obedience to authority is robust in the lab, but it has not been directly measured in ad response.
- Medicare: may say "approved to participate in Medicare" / "contracted with Medicare". May NOT claim endorsement by CMS/Medicare/HHS (422.2262(a)(1)(ix), (a)(2)(i)).
- Cite: Milgram, S. (1963). Behavioral study of obedience. *Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology, 67*(4), 371–378. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0040525

## 5. Scarcity
Limited inspection slots, limited quantities, limited-time items. **[Weak–Mixed]**
- Replication concern: the famous "cookie jar" study (Worchel et al., 1975) had small samples and has not been strongly replicated. Use only *genuine* scarcity; fake scarcity erodes trust and can be deceptive under FTC rules.
- Cite: Worchel, S., Lee, J., & Adewole, A. (1975). Effects of supply and demand on ratings of object value. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 32*(5), 906–914. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.32.5.906

## 6. Loss aversion / loss framing
"Don't let an unused coupon expire" or "Don't leave storm damage unchecked." **[Mixed]**
- Replication concern: loss aversion is foundational, but Gal & Rucker (2018) argue that many apparent loss-aversion effects disappear under other explanations. Loss framing is not reliably stronger than gain framing in persuasion, so test both.
- Removed: "storm damage claims expire 12 months after the event." Deadlines vary by policy and state, so cite the homeowner's actual policy terms instead.
- Cite: Kahneman, D., & Tversky, A. (1979). Prospect theory: An analysis of decision under risk. *Econometrica, 47*(2), 263–291. https://doi.org/10.2307/1914185
- Cite (critique): Gal, D., & Rucker, D. D. (2018). The loss of loss aversion: Will it loom larger than its gain? *Journal of Consumer Psychology, 28*(3), 497–516. https://doi.org/10.1002/jcpy.1047

## 7. Commitment & consistency
A small, self-identifying yes ("Is your roof 15+ years old? □Yes") before the main ask. See #16 for the evidence. **[Mixed]**
- Cite: Burger, J. M. (1999). The foot-in-the-door compliance procedure: A multiple-process analysis and review. *Personality and Social Psychology Review, 3*(4), 303–325. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327957pspr0304_2

## 8. Liking / similarity
A local owner's face, "family-owned in [town]". **[Mixed]**: liking increases compliance in lab work (Regan, 1971; see #2), but direct-mail effect sizes are unknown.
- Cite: Regan (1971), above.

## 9. Curiosity gap
Open an information gap on the front and resolve it on the back ("What homes on your street sold for → see back"). **[Mixed]**: a well-supported theory, but no dose-response data exists for mail.
- Removed: "$1,200/yr Medicare mistake". That figure is unverified.
- Cite: Loewenstein, G. (1994). The psychology of curiosity: A review and reinterpretation. *Psychological Bulletin, 116*(1), 75–98. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75

## 10. Urgency (real deadlines)
Concrete dates beat "act now": coupon expiration dates; Medicare's Annual Enrollment Period, **Oct 15–Dec 7** (42 CFR 422.62(a)(2)). **[Mixed]**: no single canonical study isolates this effect, so A/B test it.
- Medicare: next-year plans may be *marketed* from **Oct 1** (42 CFR 422.2263(a)). Do not send unsolicited OEP-switching mail Jan 1–Mar 31 (422.2263(b)(7)).

## 11. Zeigarnik effect / endowed progress
Pre-stamped punch cards ("2 of 10 already stamped") or "Step 1 done, call to finish." **[Mixed / Weak]**
- Replication concern: the original Zeigarnik *memory* advantage for unfinished tasks has replicated poorly. The stronger, more applicable finding is the **endowed progress effect**: car-wash cards with 2 of 10 stamps pre-filled were completed more often than 0-of-8 cards. That is a single field study and has not been widely replicated.
- Cite: Nunes, J. C., & Drèze, X. (2006). The endowed progress effect: How artificial advancement increases effort. *Journal of Consumer Research, 32*(4), 504–512. https://doi.org/10.1086/500480
- Original: Zeigarnik, B. (1927). Das Behalten erledigter und unerledigter Handlungen. *Psychologische Forschung, 9*, 1–85. (Historical; DOI not verified here.)

## 12. Decoy / choice architecture
Use Good/Better/Best tiers with an asymmetrically dominated decoy to steer toward the target option. **[Mixed]**
- Replication concern: Frederick, Lee & Baskin (2014) found the decoy effect weak or absent when options are shown with real-world (pictorial or experiential) attributes. It works best with numeric, directly comparable specs. Too many options can also lower choice (Iyengar & Lepper, 2000; that effect itself is mixed in meta-analyses), so keep it to around 3.
- Cite: Huber, J., Payne, J. W., & Puto, C. (1982). Adding asymmetrically dominated alternatives. *Journal of Consumer Research, 9*(1), 90–98. https://doi.org/10.1086/208899
- Cite: Frederick, S., Lee, L., & Baskin, E. (2014). The limits of attraction. *Journal of Marketing Research, 51*(4), 487–507. https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.12.0061
- Cite: Iyengar, S. S., & Lepper, M. R. (2000). When choice is demotivating. *JPSP, 79*(6), 995–1006. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.6.995

## 13. Color & contrast
A high-contrast CTA, and large type for older readers. **[Weak as a rule]**: no verified source supports specific color → response claims (e.g., "red = urgency lifts response"), so treat these as design hypotheses to test. Contrast and size draw attention (see #1).
- Medicare: Medicare readability standards (12-pt minimum for the plan/organization name, 422.2263(b)(9)(i)).

## 14. Von Restorff / isolation effect
One visually distinct element (a boxed offer, a sticky-note graphic) is remembered better. **[Strong]** for memory, and it is a robust lab effect.
- Do not mimic legal/government "FINAL NOTICE" styling. That is deceptive, and for Medicare it is prohibited (422.2262(a)(1)(i), (xvii)).
- Cite: von Restorff, H. (1933). Über die Wirkung von Bereichsbildungen im Spurenfeld. *Psychologische Forschung, 18*, 299–342. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02409636
- Cite: Hunt, R. R. (1995). The subtlety of distinctiveness: What von Restorff really did. *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 2*(1), 105–112. https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03214414

## 15. Specificity & price endings
Specific, verifiable claims ("Cash offer within 24 hours" — only if you actually deliver it) and price endings. **[Mixed]**: in a field test, 99-ending prices increased sales vs. 00-endings.
- Removed: "$9,850 average insurance-covered replacement" (unverified) and "odd numbers are more credible" (no verified source found).
- Cite: Schindler, R. M., & Kibarian, T. M. (1996). Increased consumer sales response though use of 99-ending prices. *Journal of Retailing, 72*(2), 187–199. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-4359(96)90013-5

## 16. Foot-in-the-door
A tiny first step (scan the QR code for a free storm map, text a keyword) and then a bigger ask. **[Mixed]**: Burger's (1999) review finds a reliable but *small* average effect that depends on conditions (e.g., the first request must be actually performed).
- Cite: Freedman, J. L., & Fraser, S. C. (1966). Compliance without pressure: The foot-in-the-door technique. *JPSP, 4*(2), 195–202. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0023552
- Cite: Burger (1999), see #7.

## 17. Endowment effect
"Your $X credit is reserved for [address]". **[Mixed]**: robust for owned physical goods in the lab; the extension to "virtual ownership" in ads is an inference. Recent critiques (Gal & Rucker, 2018) apply here too.
- Medicare: no cash or monetary rebates (422.2263(b)(1)).
- Cite: Kahneman, D., Knetsch, J. L., & Thaler, R. H. (1991). Anomalies: The endowment effect, loss aversion, and status quo bias. *Journal of Economic Perspectives, 5*(1), 193–206. https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.5.1.193

## 18. Personalization
Name, address, a photo of their street or house, timing tied to an event. **[Mixed]**: plausible and widely used, but no peer-reviewed postcard lift figure was verified.
- Removed: "variable data printing lifts response markedly" (no verified source).
- Medicare: you may NOT target by health status or income (422.2262(a)(1)(iii)–(iv)), so personalize by geography and age-in only. WBH: any reference to a homeowner's situation (probate, pre-foreclosure) must be accurate and must not imply a court, lender or government source (FTC §5; 39 USC 3001). Targeting must follow fair housing law (42 USC 3604).

## 19. Mere exposure / repeated touches
Run a consistent brand across a 3+ card sequence. **[Mixed]**
- Replication concern / frequency: the Montoya et al. (2017) meta-analysis finds liking rises with exposures and then *declines* (an inverted U), with ~10–20 exposures as the typical lab peak. That is a lab figure with simple stimuli, not a mail cadence.
- Removed: "WBH needs 5–7+ touches". That is industry folklore with no verified source.
- Cite: Zajonc, R. B. (1968). Attitudinal effects of mere exposure. *JPSP Monograph, 9*(2, Pt. 2), 1–27. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0025848
- Cite: Bornstein, R. F. (1989). Exposure and affect: Overview and meta-analysis. *Psychological Bulletin, 106*(2), 265–289. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.106.2.265
- Cite: Montoya, R. M., Horton, R. S., Vevea, J. L., Citkowicz, M., & Lauber, E. A. (2017). A re-examination of the mere exposure effect. *Psychological Bulletin, 143*(5), 459–498. https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000085

## 20. Emotion options by vertical (client's choice; no emotion favored)
These are practitioner pairings, not study claims. Any emotion, including fear, is allowed if the claims are true and compliant. See the fear appeals section in emotion-needs-madmen-report.md (EPPM: pair any threat with a clear, doable action).
- Roofing: protecting the family and home, trust, curb-appeal pride, **or** fear of storm damage, leaks or a missed insurance claim deadline (only state deadlines that exist).
- WBH: relief, speed, certainty, a fresh start, **or** fear of equity loss, repair costs or foreclosure timelines (no false "pre-approved" claims or lender look-alikes).
- Medicare: security, clarity, confidence, **or** concern about coverage gaps and out-of-pocket costs (must be accurate and not misleading or confusing, 422.2262(a)).
- Food/coupons: appetite appeal, enjoyment, smart-shopper pride (hedonic vs. utilitarian value), **or** fear of missing out on a real deadline.
- Cite: Babin, B. J., Darden, W. R., & Griffin, M. (1994). Work and/or fun: Measuring hedonic and utilitarian shopping value. *Journal of Consumer Research, 20*(4), 644–656. https://doi.org/10.1086/209376

## 21. Sell the outcome, not the feature (benefit framing)
Inspiration: Patrick's example TikTok (https://www.tiktok.com/t/ZTfMBxphD/). Core line: *"Stop selling features… sell the experience."* A cleaning company sells time, insurance sells security, real estate sells a future.
Research basis: **means-end chain theory**. Consumers link product attributes → consequences (benefits) → personal values, so messaging that climbs from the feature to the benefit and value connects to why people actually buy. **[Mixed]**: a well-established framework widely used in advertising strategy (laddering), though more descriptive than experimentally tested.
- Roofing: not "30-yr architectural shingles" but **a safe, dry home and peace of mind** every storm season.
- WBH: not "as-is cash purchase" but **relief and a fresh start**: no repairs, no showings, move on your timeline.
- Medicare: not plan specs first but **security and confidence** in coverage, or the cost of a coverage gap if the client prefers that angle. Benefit claims must be accurate and not misleading. "Free" may not describe a $0 premium (422.2262(a)(1)(xi)).
- Restaurants/food: not "16-oz ribeye" but **the moments and memories**: date night, Friday family dinner.
- Cite: Gutman, J. (1982). A means-end chain model based on consumer categorization processes. *Journal of Marketing, 46*(2), 60–72. https://doi.org/10.1177/002224298204600207

## Medicare compliance checklist (verified against eCFR text, 42 CFR Part 422 Subpart V, §§422.2260–422.2274)
Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/chapter-IV/subchapter-B/part-422/subpart-V
- No misleading/confusing content; no claim of CMS/Medicare/HHS endorsement; no misleading use of the Medicare name, CMS logo, or Medicare card image (card only with CMS authorization). §422.2262(a)(1)(i), (ix), (xvii)
- Don't use "free" for a $0 premium or reduced premium, deductibles, or cost-sharing. "Free" is allowed only for benefits at $0 cost-share for all enrollees. §422.2262(a)(1)(xi), (a)(2)(iii)
- No targeting by income or health status (outside SNP/D-SNP exceptions). §422.2262(a)(1)(iii)–(iv)
- Testimonials must come from actual enrollees, with paid endorsements and actor portrayals disclosed. §422.2262(b)
- Customer service numbers need hours of operation and a TTY number in the same font size. §422.2262(c)
- Marketing for the next plan year may start Oct 1. No cash rebates; only nominal-value gifts offered regardless of enrollment; no meals; no cross-selling non-health products. §422.2263(a)–(b)
- No unsolicited mail referencing the OEP (Jan 1–Mar 31) to enrollees. §422.2263(b)(7)
- The organization/marketing name must be shown in 12-pt font or larger, not as fine print. §422.2263(b)(9)(i)
- Third-party marketing organizations (TPMOs) selling for more than one MA organization must include the standardized TPMO disclaimer ("We do not offer every plan available in your area…"). §422.2267(e)(41)
- Agents must be licensed and appointed, and a Scope of Appointment must be documented before a personal marketing appointment. §422.2274(b)
- AEP dates: Oct 15–Dec 7. §422.62(a)(2)

## Compliance limits (the only hard rules; emotion choice belongs to the client)
- **Baseline criteria (always applies):** every generated or recommended mailer includes a sell-the-outcome option or guidance (benefits and experience over features, principle #21), whatever the emotion.
- FTC Act §5 (15 USC 45): truthful, backed-up claims; real deadlines and scarcity; FTC Endorsement Guides (16 CFR 255).
- 39 USC 3001: no government look-alikes without the required disclaimers; no fake checks or invoices.
- CMS: 42 CFR 422.2260–2274 / 423.2260–2274 (checklist above).
- Fair Housing Act 42 USC 3604(c) for real estate and WBH.
- State insurance marketing and home-solicitation rules; FTC Cooling-Off Rule 16 CFR 429 for in-home sales.
