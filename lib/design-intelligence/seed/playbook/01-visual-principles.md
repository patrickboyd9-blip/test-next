# Visual principles

Each principle has a plain explanation, rules a layout engine or a vision critic can apply, the way generated cards usually fail it, and how to critique a card for it.

Sizes below are for a 6×9 or 5×8 postcard at final trim, read at arm’s length. See `02-direct-mail.md` for the print floors.

## 1. Single focal point and dominance

**Explanation.** A card has one thing the eye hits first. Everything else is support. Dominance is not “the biggest box.” It is the thing that still reads when the card is blurred.

**Rules.**

- Exactly one dominant element. **[C]** TP-01, AF8.
- That element carries **35–50% of visual weight** (area × contrast × position). **[C]**
- The lead modality around it — the photograph, the color field, or the type field — covers **60–80%** of the front. **[C]** P1. The 35–50% element sits inside that field. Do not add the two percentages.
- The dominant element’s area is at least **2×** the next content element. **[C]** TP-01.
- Squint test: one shape remains. If two shapes tie, the card fails.

**Failure modes.** Two heroes (a giant headline and a giant price). A badge, a starburst, and a face all at display size. A 3×3 of equal cells (the offer becomes another thumbnail).

**Critique.** Blur the card to about 8 pixels of detail. Name the one remaining shape. Estimate its share of the visual punch. If you cannot name it in one phrase, or the share is under 35% or over 50% because a second shape is competing, score focal 1–2.

## 2. Hierarchy and scale

**Explanation.** People scan before they read. Size, weight, and space set the order. Color and boxes are weaker tools and get used only after scale is right (P3).

**The ladder, when the offer is the hero.**

| Role | Relationship | House target |
|---|---|---|
| Offer numeral | ≥ **1.6×** headline cap-height **[C]** | 6×9: 48–72 pt if the headline is the hero; if the numeral is the hero, it may exceed the headline and run 64–120 pt |
| Headline | ≥ **2.5×** body cap-height **[C]** AF5 | 5×8: 40–60 pt. 6×9: 48–72 pt |
| Subhead | about 0.45–0.6× the headline | one line |
| CTA / phone | ≥ 1.2× body, bold | 5×8 phone 12–16 pt; 6×9 phone 14–18 pt |
| Body | 1× | 5×8: 10–12 pt. 6×9: 11–13 pt. Floor 9 pt |
| Legal | smaller than body, never display | ≥ 7 pt **[C]** |

**When the headline is the hero** (a question, a category word), the headline is the 35–50% weight and the offer is either absent or clearly second. Do not force a 1.6× numeral onto a type-led card.

**Other rules.**

- At most **3 type sizes on the front**. **[C]** A small eyebrow may be a fourth size only if it is under 40% of the headline cap-height. Five or more sizes is a fail.
- Front order, squint test: dominant element → support line → response. Not a tie.
- One idea per size. Do not set the phone, the offer, and a second offer in the same size.

**Failure modes.** Headline, offer, and phone within 1.5× of each other (AF5). The phone larger than the offer. A legal line set in the same weight as the headline. “Free” repeated in three sizes so nothing is free-est.

**Critique.** Measure cap-height of the offer numeral and the headline in the same unit. Record the ratio. Count distinct sizes, ignoring the logo’s custom lettering. Write the order you see with the card at arm’s length. If the order is not the one the brief asked for, the hierarchy fails even if the math passes.

## 3. Balance: symmetric vs asymmetric

**Explanation.** Balance is visual weight, not mirroring. A dark photo on the left can balance a short headline on the right. Symmetry feels official, calm, institutional. Asymmetry feels like a designed object and is the default for a local-service interrupt (P2, composition notes).

**Rules.**

- Default to **asymmetric**: one heavy side (photo or numeral), one light side (a short stack). **[C]**
- Use symmetry only for a compliance-calm or ceremonial piece (Medicare explanation, a season title). Even then, one element breaks the mirror (a single price circle, a single phone).
- Optical center sits slightly above geometric center. A dominant element placed on the geometric center often feels low.
- Do not center every line. Centered stacks with equal gaps read as a template.

**Failure modes.** A three-column card with two equal offers (left dollar, right percent) and a logo in the middle. A perfectly centered headline, subhead, bullets, and button. A diagonal that exists only to look dynamic and carries no content.

**Critique.** Fold the card in half in your mind. If both halves could swap without changing the message, the piece is symmetric. Ask whether the vertical earned that. If both halves shout the same amount, it is a tie, not balance.

## 4. Composition, grids, rule of thirds

**Explanation.** A grid is the set of axes the elements share. The rule of thirds is a simple grid: two lines each way, the subject on an intersection, the quiet field in the remaining cell. One intentional break is allowed (P10). A break without a grid is just a pile.

**Rules.**

- Pick one grid and name it in the Design Rationale: `thirds`, `split-40-60`, `bands`, `full-bleed-plus-panel`, or `centered-stack`. **[C]**
- Photo or color runs to the bleed on at least two edges. **[C]** AF4. A hero inside a framed box under 25% of the card fails.
- Align every text block to the same left edge, or to the same center axis. Do not mix both on one face. **[C]**
- Gutters between modules ≥ 0.2 in. **[C]**
- At most one element breaks the grid (a price circle crossing a seam, a badge on a photo corner). A second break is clutter.

**Failure modes.** Twelve alignments. A photo in a thick white frame floating in the middle (AP-001). Modules that do not share a baseline. A “grid” of equal tiles where the offer is just another tile.

**Critique.** Draw the axes. Count how many left edges the type uses. If the count is greater than two (a main axis plus the response lockup), the grid is loose. Name the one break. If you cannot tell the break from an accident, it is not a break.

## 5. Eye path: Z, F, diagonal

**Explanation.** The path is the order a cold reader actually travels. Western readers start top-left, but a high-contrast face or numeral can steal that start. Design the path. Do not hope for it.

**Rules.**

- Name one path in the rationale: `Z`, `F`, `diagonal`, or `stacked-bands`. **[C]**
- The path has three stops and then a rest: entry (focal) → offer or promise → response. **[C]**
- Z: entry at top-left or top-right, across the headline, down the diagonal, out through the response at the opposite corner. Good for a photo with a panel.
- F: strong top line, a second shorter line, then a vertical scan of a list. Use it only when the list is the point (a proof back, not a sales front). A front that needs an F-pattern is usually too long (TP-05).
- Diagonal: the photo’s own line (a roofline, an arm, a pool edge) carries the eye into the type. The type must sit on the quiet end of that line, not on the busiest texture.
- Do not put the response where the path never goes. A QR in a corner the path does not reach will not be scanned.

**Failure modes.** Four stops of equal weight. A path that dead-ends on a logo. Type sitting in the darkest foliage at the end of a diagonal. A back that repeats the front’s path instead of switching to proof.

**Critique.** Trace the path with a finger in three seconds. Write the three stops. If the third stop is not the response, the path fails the job of the card even if it is pretty.

## 6. Gestalt: proximity, alignment, repetition

**Explanation.** Things that are close, aligned, or repeated are read as one unit. That is how a phone, a QR, and a verb become one lockup (P7). It is also how five badges become a fence.

**Rules.**

- The response lockup is one group: verb, phone, and QR within **0.5 in** of each other, sharing one alignment. **[C]** QR notes QR-1–QR-12.
- The offer is one group: numeral, one qualifier, optional real expiry. The qualifier is ≤ 40% of the numeral’s cap-height. **[C]**
- Repeat one accent color, one alignment, and one corner radius (or none). Do not repeat boxes. **[C]** TP-02.
- Space between groups ≥ 1.5× the space inside a group. **[C]** If the offer and the headline are closer to each other than the lines inside the headline, they merge and the hierarchy collapses.
- Front holds at most one group of trust (a single line). Reviews, license, and badges repeat on the back (TP-05, TP-11).

**Failure modes.** A phone at the top, a QR at the bottom, and a URL in the middle (three groups, one action). Even gaps between unrelated items. A starburst, a circle, a ribbon, and a ticket, each with its own shape language.

**Critique.** Circle the groups. There should be three to five on the front (focal, support, offer, response, mark), not twelve. Any group of one stray icon is an orphan. Orphans get cut or joined.

## 7. Contrast

**Explanation.** Contrast is the difference that makes type readable and a focal point obvious. It is luminance first, then size, then hue. Hue contrast without luminance contrast fails in a mailbox and fails for color-blind readers.

**Rules.**

- Headline, offer, and CTA ≥ **4.5:1** against the field directly behind them. **[E]** WCAG 2.x used as a print floor (AF1). House target for primary type is **≥ 7:1**. **[C]**
- Large display (≥ 24 pt) may not go below **3:1**. **[E]** The house still wants 4.5:1.
- Body ≥ 4.5:1. No reversed text under 9 pt. No weight under 400 under 14 pt. **[C]**
- Text on a photograph needs the ratio **and** a scrim, a solid panel, or a low-texture region (AF9).
- QR dark-on-light, house target ≥ 7:1, on an opaque plate if it sits over a photo. **[C]**
- One accent does the shouting. A second bright color is for a single small job (a phone, a link), not a second headline.

**Failure modes.** Gold type on a cream header. White script on a shingled roof. A yellow offer on a white card. Pale gray body on a pale photo. Two saturated colors fighting (red price and magenta phone and cyan field).

**Critique.** Sample the pixels immediately behind the headline, the offer, and the phone. Record the three ratios. Any one under 4.5:1 is an auto-fail, not a style choice.

## 8. White space and density

**Explanation.** Empty area is a tool. It tells the reader the piece is finished, and it makes the one element larger by comparison (P8). Density is how many separate facts the front asks someone to hold.

**Rules.**

- At least **25%** of the front is calm field: empty paper, sky, a flat color, or a scrimmed area with no type. **[C]** TP-08. Photo-led cards count open sky and scrim, not only blank paper.
- Front fact budget: **1** headline, **1** offer, **1** response lockup, **1** trust line, the mark. **[C]** A second offer, a service list, or a row of review logos exceeds the budget (TP-05).
- Fewer than 4 boxed modules. At most 1 starburst. **[C]** AF8, TP-02.
- Margins: live type ≥ 0.25 in inside trim. **[E]** Click2Mail safe zone.
- The back may be denser (proof, steps, fine print) and still needs one calm band so it does not become a flyer.

**Failure modes.** Edge-to-edge type. A coupon rail plus a header plus a footer plus a badge row. “White space” that is actually a pile of pale icons. Equal padding that makes every region the same importance.

**Critique.** Estimate the calm-field percentage. Count facts a stranger could repeat. More than four facts on the front means the density is too high for a glance, even if the math of the margins is fine.

## 9. Figure-ground

**Explanation.** Figure is what you see as the object. Ground is what you see as the field. A card works when the figure separates in one look: a white card on a photo, a black numeral on a yellow panel, a face against a flat wall.

**Rules.**

- The dominant element must separate from its ground by luminance, not only by a stroke or a drop shadow. **[C]**
- If type sits on a photo, the type’s box is the figure and the photo region under it is treated as ground: flatten that region (scrim or panel) until AF9 passes.
- Do not outline type to fake separation. Outlines are a template tell.
- A full-bleed photo is the ground. The figure is the offer panel or the face, not the entire photo. If the whole photo is “the figure,” add one clear figure (a panel, a word) or the card is a picture with no message.

**Failure modes.** Type the same value as the roof behind it. A busy pattern behind a paragraph. A yellow wedge, a red ribbon, and a photo all trying to be figure. A floating box with a heavy drop shadow (UI chrome, P11).

**Critique.** Convert the card to grayscale in your head. Does the figure still pop? If you need the color to find it, figure-ground is weak.

## 10. Color and emotional palettes

**Explanation.** Color sets the temperature before a word is read. Local-service mail is already loud. A short palette looks considered. A rainbow looks like a template (TP-08).

**Rules.**

- Palette: **1 neutral + 1 accent**, and at most a second accent used once. **[C]** TP-08 says ≤ 1 neutral and ≤ 2 accents. Generated cards should spend only one accent on the offer or the phone, not on both.
- Neutrals: warm paper, near-black, navy, warm gray. Accent: one of red, yellow, teal, or a brand hue. Not all of them.
- Emotional map for this business **[C]**:
  - Trust / compliance: navy, warm gray, off-white. Small accent.
  - Urgency that is real (a dated inspection window): one red or yellow used only on the date or the numeral.
  - Craft / home result: the photo’s own color, plus near-black type. Do not recolor the sky.
  - Appetite / lifestyle: the food or the material leads. Type stays white or near-black.
- No fake-official palette: no eagle-gold on slate, no “government blue” with a seal, no blackletter. **[E]** 42 USC 1320b-10 and 39 USC 3001(h) prohibit mail designed to look like a government notice. A tiny “not affiliated” line does not cure the look.
- Large fields of pure yellow are a coupon code. Use them only when the piece is honestly a coupon, and still give the numeral a darker figure.

**Failure modes.** Red, magenta, cyan, and green on one face. Gold script on parchment for a contractor. A seasonal leaf illustration in a second style from the photography. Teal used for body, headlines, and icons so nothing is accent.

**Critique.** List the hues that occupy more than about 5% of the card. More than three is too many. Check whether the accent landed on the offer or got wasted on rules and icons.

## 11. Typography: pairing, weight, measure, leading

**Explanation.** Type is the voice. One family with a clear weight change beats two display faces. Pairing is optional. A single well-chosen grotesque can carry a whole card.

**Rules.**

- One family on the front, or one grotesque plus one restrained serif for a single line. **[C]** Script is allowed as a logo, not as the headline. No stencil, no blackletter, no comic, no outlined face.
- Weights: display 700–900, body 400–500, phone 700. Do not use 300 for anything under 18 pt.
- Measure: body lines about **45–75 characters**. Headlines: **3–6 words**, hard stop at 8 (AF3). One or two lines. Deliberate line breaks. No hyphenation inside a word (AF2).
- Leading: body **120–135%** of the size. Display can be tighter (100–110%) but never colliding.
- At most 3 sizes (principle 2). Tabular lining figures for prices.
- All-caps only for a short eyebrow or a 2–3 word headline. Not for sentences. Not for legal.
- The logo is not a headline. Keep it small (about ≤ 1.25 in wide on a 6×9). **[C]**

**Failure modes.** A script headline over a photograph. Five families. The headline wrapping to four lines. Condensed type stretched to fill a box. A phone number in a light weight.

**Critique.** Name the families. Count them. Count words in the headline. Check whether any line of body is over ~75 characters. If the offer is in a script or a thin face, type fails regardless of the pairing fashion.

## 12. Photo art direction

**Explanation.** The photograph is an argument, not a decoration (P6). For a trust-blocked trade, the argument is “this crew did this work.” For a result-blocked purchase (a window, a closet, a roof), the argument is “this is what the house looks like after.”

**Rules.**

- One subject. Crop tight enough that the subject fills the frame. Full-bleed, or a panel that is ≥ 40% of the front and bleeds on at least two edges (AF4). A panel ≥ 55% is the premium target. **[C]**
- Gaze: a person looks at the work, or at the reader if the person *is* the offer (an advisor). A tech looking at the camera with a thumbs-up is a stock tell (AP-005, AP-007).
- Light: one direction, natural if it is a job site. No mixed flash-and-sun. No heavy HDR glow.
- Horizon level unless the tilt is the subject (a storm-damaged plane). Do not rotate a room relative to the type.
- Hands, tools, and the finished surface beat a portrait of a van. The van can be the hero only when the van is the recognizable local proof, and the phone still exists as type, not only as lettering on the door.
- No watermark, no clip-art insects, no extra illustration style beside the photo.
- Color grade toward the palette. Do not let the photo introduce a fourth hue family.
- Before/after: the after is large, the before is a small labeled inset. Two equal frames bury the result.

**Failure modes.** A stock family in a kitchen. A collage of six services. Lightning bolts stocked over a house. Subject eyes closed or cut off at the chin. Text across the face. A framed stock box at 10% of the card.

**Critique.** Say what the photo claims in one sentence. If the sentence is “a nice house” and the product is a roof, the crop failed. If you cannot tell whether it is this contractor’s work, it will not carry trust.
