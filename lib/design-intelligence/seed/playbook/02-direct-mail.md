# Direct mail

A postcard is a physical interrupt in a stack of bills. The principles in `01-visual-principles.md` still apply. These rules are the extra constraints of the mailbox, the press, and the postal rules.

## 13. The 3-second glance test

**Explanation.** The first look happens over a trash can. Three seconds is the whole brief. If the card cannot deliver category, offer, and next step in that window, the rest of the craft does not matter.

**Rules.**

- A passing glance recalls exactly three things: what it is, what you get, how to respond. **[C]**
- The front carries one offer and a headline of ≤ 8 words, target 3–6 (AF3, TP-05).
- The dominant element alone must communicate the category or the offer. A logo does not count as the category.
- Do not require the reader to turn the card over to learn the offer. The back proves and redeems. It does not introduce the deal (P5).

**Failure modes.** A beautiful photograph and a logo, offer on the back. A headline that is a service list (“Roofing • Siding • Gutters”). Four prices of equal size. A card that recalls a mood and not an action.

**Critique.** Show the front for three seconds, cover it, and write what you remember. Score 5 only if all three parts are present and correct. Score 2 if you remember a color and a logo. This is the glance_3s score in the analyses.

## 14. Front vs back roles

**Explanation.** People handle a card in an order: front interrupt, flip for proof, then decide whether to call or scan (P5). The two faces are one composition with two jobs, not two ads.

**Rules.**

- Front: one focal element, one offer, one response lockup, one trust line, the mark. No service-bullet list (TP-05).
- Back: proof (one job story, three steps, or a short review), the offer repeated once so the card still works if they flip first, the response lockup repeated, legal, and the address panel.
- The back uses the same type family, palette, and grid as the front (TP-10). It may be denser.
- If only one face is designed, it must be the front, and it must contain the offer and the response. A silent brand face fails as mail even when it is beautiful.
- Address side: no marketing type inside the USPS clear zone (principle 20). A back that is only an address with a diagonal slogan is not a proof face.

**Failure modes.** The same headline on both sides. Proof badges crammed on the front. The offer existing only as “see back.” A front and back that look like different companies.

**Critique.** Cover one face. Can you still say what to do? Then cover the other. Does the hidden face add proof rather than a second campaign? If both faces could be mailed alone as different ads, the roles failed.

## 15. Offer architecture

**Explanation.** An offer is a number plus the conditions, or it is a concrete next step with no discount (TP-04). “Special savings” is not an offer. Two offers are a second ad.

**Rules.**

- One offer on the front. **[C]**
- If it has a number, the numeral is the largest or second-largest type (TP-04). When the offer is the hero, numeral cap-height ≥ 1.6× the headline. **[C]**
- One qualifier directly under the numeral, ≤ 40% of the numeral’s cap-height: “per window,” “off the inspection,” “for new customers.” **[C]**
- An expiry appears only when it is real, and only once, smaller than the qualifier. Fake deadlines and “respond within 5 days” on a lookalike notice are forbidden. **[E]** deceptive-mail rules; see Medicare in `03-verticals.md`.
- Financing, a second trade, or a bonus gift is a different card or a back-line, not a second hero.
- Premium tier may skip the discount and lead with the concrete step (a free inspection, a named consultation). Do not invent a percent to satisfy the template.

**Failure modes.** Three prices in one rail. “$500 off” and “10% off” at the same size. A starburst with a different deal from the headline. The price explained only in 6 pt type.

**Critique.** Count offers a stranger could redeem. More than one on the front fails. Check that the number’s conditions are attached to the number, not stranded in a footer.

## 16. CTA and response devices

**Explanation.** The response is the product. A card that cannot be acted on is a poster. Phone and QR are one lockup, with one of them dominant (P7, P9, TP-07, TP-09).

**Rules.**

- Exactly one primary action. The other channel is visibly smaller and inside the same group. **[C]**
- Phone ≥ 11 pt, house target 12–16 pt on 5×8 and 14–18 pt on 6×9, bold, with separators. **[C]** AF7.
- QR ≥ 0.75 in, target 0.9–1.0 in, quiet zone of 4 modules, dark on a light opaque plate, ≥ 3:1 and house target ≥ 7:1. One QR per face. A verb label within 0.5 in (“Scan to book the inspection”). **[E]** ISO/IEC 18004 quiet zone. **[C]** QR-1–QR-12.
- Payload short, on the order of 40 characters, and it must resolve. **[C]**
- No pill button, no drop shadow, no fake app chrome (P11, AP-008). The CTA is type plus the device, not a website button photographed onto paper.
- Do not rely on a phone number that exists only as lettering on a truck in the photo.
- Scan-distance rule of thumb: code width on the order of distance/10. A 1 in code is for a close look, not a wall. **[C]** industry practice, not a postal spec.

**Failure modes.** A bare QR in a corner. Three phones. A URL, a QR, and a coupon code as equal heroes. A button that says “Learn more” with no offer. Two QR codes.

**Critique.** Put a finger on the one action. If you hesitate, there isn’t one. Measure the QR and the phone. Check the quiet zone visually: any type inside the four-module margin fails the code even if the artwork looks balanced.

## 17. Trust signals

**Explanation.** Trust is evidence that this sender is real and local. It is not a wall of badges. One proof, subordinate to the offer, beats six logos (TP-11).

**Rules.**

- Front: at most one trust line, smaller than the offer and the phone. Examples: years in the town, a license number, one review count. **[C]**
- Badges, manufacturer marks, and review-site logos move to the back, in one row, each smaller than the company mark (TP-11).
- The strongest trust for a trade is the photograph of the work, not an icon (P6).
- Do not borrow federal or state seals, the flag as a graphic system, or “official notice” language. **[E]**
- A review count needs a real source in the data. Do not invent stars.
- Personal names and license numbers of real agents in reference photos are not reusable. They are evidence of the pattern “a real local face,” not a portrait library.

**Failure modes.** A row of five seals across the headline. “Board certified” with no name. A stock handshake. Government-looking folio numbers. A tiny disclaimer under a piece that otherwise impersonates an agency.

**Critique.** Cover the badges. Does the card still feel trustworthy because of the photo, the offer clarity, and the local phone? If trust collapses without the badges, the badges were doing the design’s job and the design failed.

## 18. The mailbox context

**Explanation.** The card arrives in a stack, often under a bill, often in poor light, often glanced by someone standing up. It competes with white envelopes. A quiet, high-contrast figure beats a noisy full-bleed pattern because the stack itself is noisy.

**Rules.**

- The outer 0.5 in of a card is often covered by a thumb or the next piece. Do not put the only phone number there. **[C]**
- Assume the first sight is the top third. Either the focal element enters the top half, or the top half clearly leads into it (a roof running to an offer below). **[C]**
- Design for a mixed stack: the piece should still read if a neighboring card is red, white, or kraft. Dependence on a delicate pastel edge is fragile.
- One card, one job, one audience moment (TP-12). A storm-hit owner and a new mover do not share a headline.
- Series: same grid, type, and palette as the company’s other pieces so the second card is recognized (TP-10). Variation happens inside the photo, headline, and offer slots.

**Failure modes.** A design that only works flat on a lightbox. Edge-to-edge microtype. A different palette on every drop. Humor with no offer and no brand recognition.

**Critique.** Lay the card on a white envelope and a colored envelope in your mind. Does the figure still separate? Is the action still in the uncovered center? If the piece only works as a full-frame photograph on a table, it is not finished as mail.

## 19. Reading distance and minimum print sizes

**Explanation.** Sorting distance is about 18–24 inches. Reading distance, if the card survives, is about 12–16 inches. Type that looks fine on a monitor at 100% zoom is often small in the hand. These floors match the rubric’s print table.

**Rules.**

| Element | 5×8 minimum | 6×9 minimum | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Headline | 40 pt | 48 pt | 3–6 words |
| Offer numeral, when it is the hero | ≥ 1.6× the headline cap-height | same | May exceed the headline band |
| Body | 10 pt | 11 pt | Floor 9 pt; leading 120–135% |
| Phone | 12 pt bold | 14 pt bold | AF7 floor is 11 pt |
| CTA verb | 16 pt | 18 pt | Next to the device |
| Legal | 7 pt | 7 pt | Never reversed out of a photo |
| QR | 0.75 in | 0.75 in | Target 0.9–1.0 in |
| Logo | ≤ about 1.25 in wide | same | Not the headline |

- Effective image resolution ≥ 300 ppi at the placed size. **[E]**
- Do not set a headline by screen appearance. Set it in points on the trim size.

**Failure modes.** A 24 pt headline on a 6×9. Body at 8 pt. A QR at 0.4 in. A phone that is large on screen because the artboard was zoomed in, and small on the trim.

**Critique.** Confirm the artboard is the trim size plus bleed, then read point sizes from the layout, not from a screenshot. Anything under the floor fails even if the hierarchy ratios are fashionable.

## 20. USPS zones and postal facts

**Explanation.** The press and the postal service remove a margin and reserve a corner. Design inside that, or the card is non-machinable, rejected, or looks illegal.

**Rules.** **[E]** unless marked [C]. Geometry matches `rubrics/A-Plus-Postcard-Rubric.md`, verified 2026-10-09.

| | 5×8 | 6×9 |
|---|---|---|
| Trim | 5 × 8 in | 6 × 9 in |
| Artboard with 0.25 in bleed | 5.5 × 8.5 in | 6.5 × 9.5 in |
| Safe zone (live type ≥ 0.25 in inside trim) | 4.5 × 7.5 in | 5.5 × 8.5 in |
| Pixels at 300 dpi including bleed | 1650 × 2550 | 1950 × 2850 |

- Photos and background color extend to the bleed. Type, QR, and logos stay in the safe zone (AF6).
- Address side: keep the indicia, address block, and barcode clear. USPS barcode clear zone on machinable letter-size mail: **4.75 in from the right × 0.625 in from the bottom** (DMM 202.5.1.1), unless the Intelligent Mail barcode sits in the address block. **[E]** Use the Click2Mail template as the production mask.
- First-Class Mail card price: postcards from 3.5 × 5 in up to **6 × 9 in**, thickness 0.007–0.016 in (≥ 0.009 in if over 4.25 × 6), aspect ratio 1.3–2.5 (DMM 201). A 6×11 is a letter, not a card.
- USPS Marketing Mail prices cards as letters. There is no card rate (DMM 243).
- Do not place a QR, a coupon, or a headline in the clear zone to “use the space.”
- Government-look prohibition stands even when the zone math is perfect.

**Failure modes.** Type in the bleed. A QR in the barcode corner. A 6×11 laid out as if it were a postcard rate. Art that collides with the address block on the back.

**Critique.** Overlay the safe box and the clear zone. Every live element is inside the safe box. The clear zone is empty of ink except the postal marks the template owns. This is a pass/fail, not a 1–5 score.
